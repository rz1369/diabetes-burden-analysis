Why is diabetes getting worse, and does it hurt the economy?

My master's thesis in plain language · Ruhollah Zamaninia · M.Sc. Economic Policy, University of Siegen, 2026 · Grade 1.0

Diabetes is one of the main chronic diseases in the world, and it is getting worse. Between 2000 and 2023, the average burden of diabetes across countries rose by almost 9 percent. For my master's thesis I wanted to find out what is behind that rise, and whether it costs countries economic growth.

I collected data for 191 countries over 24 years. To measure how much harm diabetes does, I used a standard measure from global health research: the number of healthy years of life lost to diabetes, through early death or living with complications such as kidney failure, blindness or amputation. I then put this next to economic and population data from the World Bank.

1. What makes diabetes worse in a country?

Rising obesity and people moving to cities. Higher income and more health spending did not make a clear difference.

At first sight the data say the opposite about cities. Countries where most people live in cities tend to have less diabetes. But that is mainly because those countries are also richer and have better health care.

So instead of comparing countries with each other, I compared each country with itself over time. Seen that way the picture flips: when a country becomes more urban, its own diabetes burden goes up. City life tends to mean more sitting, less walking and more processed food.

Getting richer or spending more on health did not, by itself, lower diabetes harm from one year to the next. That does not mean money is useless. It suggests that the advantage of richer countries builds up over decades rather than year by year, and that more spending on treatment alone should not be expected to bring the burden down quickly.

2. Does diabetes slow down the economy?

I could not find a measurable effect.

I checked whether countries with more diabetes in one year grow more slowly the next year. They don't, at least not in a way the data can show. This is not the same as saying diabetes has no economic cost. Diabetes changes slowly, while economic growth jumps around from year to year, so a small or slow effect is very hard to see. My data could only have picked up a very large one.

3. What about Germany?

Care for adults is getting better, but more children are getting type 2 diabetes, and the East still does worse than the West.

Germany loses fewer healthy years to diabetes than most countries, but more than its neighbours France, the Netherlands and Denmark, even though it spends a lot on health.

Using data from the Robert Koch-Institut, I looked inside the country. Type 2 diabetes, the kind linked to weight and lifestyle, is still rare in children, but it is becoming more common. Among German children and teenagers it rose by about 61 percent between 2014 and 2022.

At the same time, things are improving for adults. Between 2015 and 2022, amputations caused by diabetes fell by 11 percent and avoidable hospital stays by 26 percent. Between 2013 and 2022, new disability pensions because of diabetes fell by more than a third.

There is also a clear regional gap. The five eastern states have the most avoidable hospital stays for diabetes. Every state improved between 2016 and 2022, and the East caught up somewhat, but the gap is still there.

What this means

Treating people who already have diabetes is working better, at least in Germany. But the number of people at risk keeps growing, and the strongest links in my data are with obesity and city life. That points towards prevention: healthier food, more movement in daily life, and cities designed for walking and cycling. The rise among children makes this more urgent.

How I did it, briefly

The diabetes figures come from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023, the economic and population data from the World Bank, and the German figures from the Robert Koch-Institut. I analysed them in Stata, following each country over time so that fixed differences between countries, like geography or history, do not distort the results. The results show links, not proof of cause and effect, so the calculator above is an illustration, not a forecast. The thesis was supervised by Prof. Dr. Lars Siemers at the University of Siegen.
